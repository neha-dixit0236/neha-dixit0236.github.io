NYC Taxi Data Ethics

Privacy and the Risks of Data De-Anonymization
Author

Neha Dixit

Published

November 25, 2026

Introduction

In 2014, New York City officials received a Freedom of Information Law request from Chris Whong, who asked for taxi trip data in 2013. Thus, city officials released a dataset containing information about approximately 173 million taxi trips, but not without attempting to anonymize the data first. To protect the privacy of the taxi drivers in the dataset, officials coded the license plate and medallion numbers for each trip before making the information public. However, the predictable nature of license plate and medallion numbers in taxis in New York meant that these coded values were vulnerable to de-anonymization. Vijay Pandurangan proved this in June 2014, when he reversed the anonymization process in less than two hours and was able to link trips back to specific drivers (Goodin, 2014).

This incident only became more controversial when, in October 2014, Northwestern graduate student Anthony Tockar, pointed out that this dataset could reveal information about the passengers in these taxi trips as well (Gawker, 2014). Using the timestamps of paparazzi photos of celebrities entering and exiting taxis, Tockar was able to cross reference the taxi trip dataset to deduce pickup and drop-off locations, the amount of the fare, and make educated guesses regarding how much each celebrity tipped their driver.

This situation highlights several important conversations surrounding data privacy, anonymity, and the responsibility of organizations when collecting and sharing large amounts of information. While it is important to have public data channels to further research, it is also important to ensure that the anonymization methods used by organizations or government agencies when releasing this data is secure.

Prior Research

Dan Goodin’s Ars Technica Article

The first article I looked at was Dan Goodin’s 2014 article, “Poorly Anonymized Logs Reveal NYC Cab Drivers’ Detailed Whereabouts,” published by Ars Technica. Goodin explains that New York City made over 173 million taxi trip records publicly available after first attempting to protect the privacy of the taxi drivers in these trips. To anonymize the data, the city used a technique called hashing to replace identifiable information like taxi license and medallion numbers with coded values instead. However, Vijay Pandurangan, a software engineer, realized that license plate numbers and medallion numbers are not random strings of digits. Rather, they follow a predictable pattern. By researching this pattern and the MD5 hashing algorithm used to anonymize the data, Pandurangan used computer software to generate possible license and medallion numbers and compare their hashes with the released dataset. Thus, in less than two hours, Pandurangan was able to de-anonymize the entire taxi trip dataset. This meant that individuals could use the in-depth description of all routes taxi cabs took throughout 2013 to potentially identify specific drivers. Additionally, with this location data, it became possible for these individuals to estimate driver earnings, passengers of these trips, and even where the drivers lived.

The article also highlights that the use of the MD5 hashing algorithm was not at fault for the vulnerability of the dataset. Rather, Pandurangan argued that the issue arose from the way the city officials chose to anonymize the information. He noted that officials could have better protected driver privacy by assigning random identification numbers to each driver and taxi, or by using AES encryption with a secret key. These two methods would have made it much harder to reverse the anonymization process and identify the specific drivers. While Pandurangan acknowledged that it would be impossible for New York City officials to have made these changes after releasing the data (since copies of this data had already spread to numerous places), he highlighted that more secure data protection methods needed to be implemented in the future to prevent sensitive information from becoming publicly available.

Vijay Pandurangan’s “On Taxis and Rainbows”

The second article I looked at was an article Vijay Pandurangan wrote on Medium.com titled “On Taxis and Rainbows,” which provided more detail into how he was able to de-anonymize such a large dataset. First, the article explains the nuances of the MD5 hashing function. Like all hashing functions, the MD5 algorithm is deterministic, meaning it always produces the exact same hash value for the same input, regardless of how many times the same number is inputted or where it is run. The reason these hash functions are used to anonymize data is because, given the hash value (the output), it is very difficult to determine what the original input is. However, there is an important caveat. If an individual knows something about what the input might look like, the deterministic nature of hash functions makes the anonymized data vulnerable to reverse algorithms, allowing people to uncover the true inputs.

Pandurangan then described the patterns he saw in taxi license plate numbers and medallions. In New York City, taxi license numbers are 6 or 7 digit numbers. Additionally, he realized all 7 digit taxi license plates started with a 5. Thus, Pandurangan was able to conclude that there were only about 3 million possible taxi license numbers. He notes that medallion numbers also followed three specific patterns: one number, one letter, and two numbers, two letters and three numbers, or three letters and three numbers. Thus, Pandurangan found that there are about 19 million possible medallion numbers. With this and the license plate pattern, Pandurangan had 22 million strings of numbers that represented all of the possible inputs the city officials provided to the MD5 algorithm. Therefore, by generating the MD5 hashes for all of these numbers and comparing them to the values in the dataset, he was able to reverse the entire anonymization.

Pandurangan explained that generating the hashes took less than two minutes, meaning the entire reversing process took him less than two hours using multiple computers. After explaining how easily the data could be de-anonymized, Pandurangan suggested two alternative methods that could have better protected the taxi drivers’ identifiable information. First, he suggested assigning a completely random identification number to each taxi license and medallion number and consistently reusing these numbers throughout the dataset. Implementing this randomization would allow researchers to track the same taxi/driver across multiple trips, meaning the dataset could still be used for research purposes, while also making sure the original license and medallion numbers weren’t revealed. More importantly, because these replacement numbers would be random as opposed to based on a predictable pattern, individuals would not be abe to generate a list of possible inputs to match their hashes to the dataset, thus making the MD5 hashing algorithm much more secure. Pandurangan’s next suggestion was to create a secret AES encryption key and use the secret key to encrypt each license and medallion number individually. Using secret keys to encrypt data means that individuals would have no way of accessing the original license and medallion data without access to the key itself. Thus, unlike the MD5 hashing algorithm, using keys would make it much more difficult for the drivers’ sensitive information to get leaked, and the dataset would still be useful for research purposes.
